## Problem and evidence

When an individual customer of a European retail bank is about to buy shares in a US company, such as Coca-Cola, they go through their bank's app. They are not a professional investor. They may have little more than the company's name, its share price or something they read online, and they do not want to spend much time on it. Before placing the order, they want a quick, objective first look at the company, based on figures taken from its annual reports, without reading the full report.

In the United States, companies listed on a stock exchange file an annual report every year (the "10-K"), which is free and accessible to everyone on the SEC website (EDGAR). This report contains the company's complete accounts (balance sheet, income statement, etc.). Yet these reports are rarely downloaded from the SEC website: between 2003 and 2012, a listed company's annual report was requested on average only 28.4 times* by investors right after it was published (Loughran and McDonald, 2017). One of the reasons may be their length: by 2014, they were more than six times longer than in 1995 (Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen, 2020, as summarised by the NBER Digest, November 2018). For a non-professional European investor, it is not realistic to read such a document before each stock purchase, especially since these reports are in English.

\* This figure only includes downloads from the SEC website. Professionals also read these reports via fee-paying terminals.

So, these investors get their information elsewhere and often have limited financial knowledge. Among the youngest, 48% of US Gen Z investors learn about investing mainly through social media (FINRA Foundation and CFA Institute, 2023). In the United States, 26% of investors say they use recommendations from social media influencers when making investment decisions; this rises to 61% of those aged 18–34 and 57% of those with less than two years of investing experience (Lin et al., 2025). On average, US investors answer only 5.3 of 11 questions correctly in an investor knowledge quiz (Lin et al., 2025), and only 18% of EU citizens have a high level of financial literacy (European Commission, 2023). Therefore, many investors make decisions without looking at the company's own figures.

This problem has a real cost. First, it is a cost for the customer. Attention-driven buying can be costly: when users of the Robinhood app (a US brokerage app) buy a stock most heavily on a given day, that stock then has an average abnormal return of −4.7% over the following 20 days (Barber et al., 2022). Moreover, 41% of Gen Z investors in the US and Canada cite fear of missing out as a factor in starting to invest (FINRA Foundation and CFA Institute, 2023). It is also a cost in time: looking for figures and checking information online takes time that not everyone has, especially if their stock portfolio is large. Finally, it is a cost for the bank: it wants to help its customers understand what they buy without giving investment advice, so it needs a neutral tool it can offer next to its order screen.

The customer therefore needs a simple, sourced view of a company's accounts at the moment they buy its shares. Investors also prefer information presented in a visual format, with bullets, charts and graphs (SEC, 2012). The next section presents our solution.

These studies show that the problem exists, not that customers will use our app; most of them describe US investors and some are more than a decade old (see the sources annex).
